Introduction

In Part 5, we introduced the image of the absolute conic,

\[ \omega = K^{-\top}K^{-1}, \]

and saw that once \(\omega\) is known, the intrinsic matrix \(K\) can be recovered, for example through a Cholesky factorization.

But one question remained - How do we estimate \(\omega\) from actual images?

This is the camera calibration problem.

The basic idea is simple. We photograph a flat pattern whose geometry is already known, observe how the camera distorts that pattern, and use those measurements to infer the unknown entries of \(K\).

Because the pattern is planar, each image of it is related to the physical plane by a homography. The rest of the post is about how those homographies encode the Euclidean constraints needed to recover the camera intrinsics.

Camera Calibration

What is the problem?

A pinhole camera maps a world point to an image point through

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf x} = K \begin{bmatrix} R & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \widetilde{\mathbf X}, \]

followed by the perspective division

\[ \mathbf x = \phi\left(\widetilde{\mathbf x}\right). \]

The intrinsic matrix

\[ K = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & s & p_x \\ 0 & f_y & p_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

contains the internal parameters of the camera:

  • the horizontal and vertical focal lengths \(f_x\) and \(f_y\),
  • the skew \(s\),
  • the principal point \((p_x,p_y)\).

The rotation matrix \(R\) and the translation vector \(\mathbf t\) describe the pose of the camera relative to the scene. These may change from one image to another. The intrinsic matrix \(K\), however, remains fixed as long as the internal camera settings remain unchanged.

The camera calibration problem is therefore:

Given image measurements of points whose geometry is known, estimate the intrinsic matrix \(K\).

For now, we focus only on the ideal pinhole camera. Lens distortion will be introduced later.

Suppose the known points lie on a plane. We choose the world coordinate system so that this plane is

\[ Z=0. \]

A point on the plane therefore has coordinates

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf X} = \begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y\\ 0\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Write the rotation matrix as

\[ R = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf r_3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf x} = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf r_3 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y\\ 0\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Because the third world coordinate is zero, the term involving \(\mathbf r_3\) disappears:

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf x} = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Define

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi} = \begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf x} = H\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi}, \]

where

\[ H = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix}. \]

After applying the map \(\phi\), we obtain the Euclidean image point:

\[ \mathbf x = \phi\left(H\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi}\right). \]

Thus every image of the known plane gives us a homography.

The calibration problem has now changed form:

Estimate one or more homographies from image correspondences, and use them to recover the intrinsic matrix \(K\).

Wait, have we not already recovered camera parameters from a homography?

Earlier, when studying the full camera matrix, we estimated

\[ \widehat P = \begin{bmatrix} \widehat M & \widehat{\mathbf p}_4 \end{bmatrix}, \]

where the left \(3\times 3\) block satisfies

\[ M=KR. \]

This factorization has a useful structure:

  • \(K\) is upper triangular,
  • \(R\) is orthogonal.

This allows us to recover \(K\) and \(R\) from the full camera matrix using an RQ-type factorization, followed by the appropriate scale and sign corrections.

A planar homography is also a \(3\times 3\) matrix, but its structure is different:

\[ H = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix}. \]

The matrix

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \]

is not a rotation matrix. Its first two columns are rotation columns, but its third column is the translation vector.

In general,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix}^{\top} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \neq I. \]

Therefore, we cannot simply decompose \(H\) into an upper-triangular matrix and an orthogonal matrix and interpret the factors as \(K\) and \(R\).

The useful information inside \(H\) comes from the fact that \(\mathbf r_1\) and \(\mathbf r_2\) are columns of a rotation matrix. Hence,

\[ \mathbf r_1^{\top}\mathbf r_2=0, \]

and

\[ \|\mathbf r_1\|_2 = \|\mathbf r_2\|_2 = 1. \]

These Euclidean constraints allow the homography to tell us something about \(K\).

Why is one homography not enough?

Suppose a homography has been estimated from point correspondences:

\[ \widehat H = \begin{bmatrix} \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 & \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 & \widehat{\mathbf h}_3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The point correspondences determine the homography only up to a common nonzero multiplier.

If \(\widehat H\) produces the correct image points, then so does

\[ c\widehat H, \qquad c\neq 0, \]

because

\[ \phi\left(c\widehat H\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi}\right) = \phi\left(\widehat H\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi}\right). \]

Therefore, the estimated matrix is related to

\[ H = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \]

by some unknown nonzero scalar \(c\):

\[ \widehat H = cK \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix}. \]

Looking at the first two columns,

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 = cK\mathbf r_1, \]

and

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = cK\mathbf r_2. \]

Multiplying by \(K^{-1}\) gives

\[ K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_1 = c\mathbf r_1, \]

and

\[ K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = c\mathbf r_2. \]

The vectors on the left are therefore equally scaled versions of two orthonormal rotation columns.

Because \(\mathbf r_1\) and \(\mathbf r_2\) are orthogonal,

\[ \left(K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_1\right)^{\top} \left(K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_2\right) = 0. \]

Expanding,

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} K^{-\top}K^{-1} \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0. \]

Using

\[ \omega = K^{-\top}K^{-1}, \]

we obtain

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0. \]

This is exactly why we developed the absolute conic and its image: they turn the Euclidean structure hidden inside the camera into algebraic constraints on the unknown intrinsics.

This is the first constraint supplied by the homography.

Next, since \(\mathbf r_1\) and \(\mathbf r_2\) are both unit vectors,

\[ \|c\mathbf r_1\|_2^2 = \|c\mathbf r_2\|_2^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \left\|K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_1\right\|_2^2 = \left\|K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_2\right\|_2^2. \]

Expanding again,

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} K^{-\top}K^{-1} \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 = \widehat{\mathbf h}_2^{\top} K^{-\top}K^{-1} \widehat{\mathbf h}_2. \]

Hence,

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 - \widehat{\mathbf h}_2^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0. \]

This is the second constraint.

Thus one homography gives two equations involving \(\omega\):

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0, \]

and

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 - \widehat{\mathbf h}_2^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0. \]

Now recall that \(\omega\) is symmetric:

\[ \omega = \begin{pmatrix} \omega_{11} & \omega_{12} & \omega_{13}\\ \omega_{12} & \omega_{22} & \omega_{23}\\ \omega_{13} & \omega_{23} & \omega_{33} \end{pmatrix}. \]

It has six entries. However, the equations used to estimate it are homogeneous, so \(\omega\) can initially be recovered only up to a common nonzero scale. It therefore has five independent degrees of freedom.

One homography provides only two constraints.

Therefore, a single planar image does not contain enough information to recover a completely general intrinsic matrix.

A second image of the same plane, viewed from a different pose, produces another homography and two more constraints. A third sufficiently different view produces two more.

The intrinsic matrix \(K\) remains the same across all views, while the rotation and translation change:

\[ H_i = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_{1i} & \mathbf r_{2i} & \mathbf t_i \end{bmatrix}. \]

Each new homography contributes two equations involving the same matrix \(\omega\).

This is the central idea of planar camera calibration.
