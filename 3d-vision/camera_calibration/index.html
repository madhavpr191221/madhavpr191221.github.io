Introduction

In Part 5, we introduced the image of the absolute conic,

\[ \omega = K^{-\top}K^{-1}, \]

and saw that once \(\omega\) is known, the intrinsic matrix \(K\) can be recovered, for example through a Cholesky factorization.

But one question remained - How do we estimate \(\omega\) from actual images?

This is the camera calibration problem.

The basic idea is simple. We photograph a flat pattern whose geometry is already known, observe how the camera distorts that pattern, and use those measurements to infer the unknown entries of \(K\).

Because the pattern is planar, each image of it is related to the physical plane by a homography. The rest of the post is about how those homographies encode the Euclidean constraints needed to recover the camera intrinsics.

Camera Calibration

What is the problem?

A pinhole camera maps a world point to an image point through

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf x} = K \begin{bmatrix} R & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \widetilde{\mathbf X}, \]

followed by the perspective division

\[ \mathbf x = \phi\left(\widetilde{\mathbf x}\right). \]

The intrinsic matrix

\[ K = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & s & p_x \\ 0 & f_y & p_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

contains the internal parameters of the camera:

  • the horizontal and vertical focal lengths \(f_x\) and \(f_y\),
  • the skew \(s\),
  • the principal point \((p_x,p_y)\).

The rotation matrix \(R\) and the translation vector \(\mathbf t\) describe the pose of the camera relative to the scene. These may change from one image to another. The intrinsic matrix \(K\), however, remains fixed as long as the internal camera settings remain unchanged.

The camera calibration problem is therefore:

Given image measurements of points whose geometry is known, estimate the intrinsic matrix \(K\).

For now, we focus only on the ideal pinhole camera. Lens distortion will be introduced later.

Suppose the known points lie on a plane. We choose the world coordinate system so that this plane is

\[ Z=0. \]

A point on the plane therefore has coordinates

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf X} = \begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y\\ 0\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Write the rotation matrix as

\[ R = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf r_3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf x} = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf r_3 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y\\ 0\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Because the third world coordinate is zero, the term involving \(\mathbf r_3\) disappears:

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf x} = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Define

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi} = \begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf x} = H\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi}, \]

where

\[ H = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix}. \]

After applying the map \(\phi\), we obtain the Euclidean image point:

\[ \mathbf x = \phi\left(H\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi}\right). \]

Thus every image of the known plane gives us a homography.

The calibration problem has now changed form:

Estimate one or more homographies from image correspondences, and use them to recover the intrinsic matrix \(K\).

The planar calibration target

To estimate a homography, we need several known points on the plane and their corresponding image locations.

A common choice is a flat checkerboard pattern made of alternating dark and light squares. The useful features are not the squares themselves, but the inner corners where four neighbouring squares meet.

Suppose each square has side length \(d\). We may choose one inner corner as the origin and assign plane coordinates

\[ \mathbf X_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} id\\ jd \end{pmatrix}, \]

where \(i\) and \(j\) index the corner horizontally and vertically.

In homogeneous coordinates,

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf X}_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} id\\ jd\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The camera observes each of these corners at some image location

\[ \mathbf x_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} u_{ij}\\ v_{ij} \end{pmatrix}. \]

Thus one image provides a collection of correspondences

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf X}_{ij} \longleftrightarrow \mathbf x_{ij}. \]

These correspondences are used to estimate the homography between the physical plane and the image.

The choice of origin on the pattern is arbitrary. The only requirement is that the relative geometry of the points is known.

For estimating the intrinsic matrix, we may even choose

\[ d=1. \]

The actual physical square size is needed only if we want quantities such as the translation vector to be recovered in physical units.

The checkerboard therefore serves a simple purpose:

\[ \text{known planar corner coordinates} + \text{measured image coordinates} \longrightarrow \widehat H. \]

By photographing the same planar pattern from several different poses, we obtain

\[ \widehat H_1, \widehat H_2, \ldots, \widehat H_m. \]

These homographies are then used to recover the common intrinsic matrix of the camera.

Wait, have we not already recovered camera parameters from a homography?

Earlier, when studying the full camera matrix, we estimated

\[ \widehat P = \begin{bmatrix} \widehat M & \widehat{\mathbf p}_4 \end{bmatrix}, \]

where the left \(3\times 3\) block satisfies

\[ M=KR. \]

This factorization has a useful structure:

  • \(K\) is upper triangular,
  • \(R\) is orthogonal.

This allows us to recover \(K\) and \(R\) from the full camera matrix using an RQ-type factorization, followed by the appropriate scale and sign corrections.

A planar homography is also a \(3\times 3\) matrix, but its structure is different:

\[ H = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix}. \]

The matrix

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \]

is not a rotation matrix. Its first two columns are rotation columns, but its third column is the translation vector.

In general,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix}^{\top} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \neq I. \]

Therefore, we cannot simply decompose \(H\) into an upper-triangular matrix and an orthogonal matrix and interpret the factors as \(K\) and \(R\).

The useful information inside \(H\) comes from the fact that \(\mathbf r_1\) and \(\mathbf r_2\) are columns of a rotation matrix. Hence,

\[ \mathbf r_1^{\top}\mathbf r_2=0, \]

and

\[ \|\mathbf r_1\|_2 = \|\mathbf r_2\|_2 = 1. \]

These Euclidean constraints allow the homography to tell us something about \(K\).

Why is one homography not enough?

Suppose a homography has been estimated from point correspondences:

\[ \widehat H = \begin{bmatrix} \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 & \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 & \widehat{\mathbf h}_3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The point correspondences determine the homography only up to a common nonzero multiplier.

If \(\widehat H\) produces the correct image points, then so does

\[ c\widehat H, \qquad c\neq 0, \]

because

\[ \phi\left(c\widehat H\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi}\right) = \phi\left(\widehat H\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{\pi}\right). \]

Therefore, the estimated matrix is related to

\[ H = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix} \]

by some unknown nonzero scalar \(c\):

\[ \widehat H = cK \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_1 & \mathbf r_2 & \mathbf t \end{bmatrix}. \]

Looking at the first two columns,

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 = cK\mathbf r_1, \]

and

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = cK\mathbf r_2. \]

Multiplying by \(K^{-1}\) gives

\[ K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_1 = c\mathbf r_1, \]

and

\[ K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = c\mathbf r_2. \]

The vectors on the left are therefore equally scaled versions of two orthonormal rotation columns.

Because \(\mathbf r_1\) and \(\mathbf r_2\) are orthogonal,

\[ \left(K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_1\right)^{\top} \left(K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_2\right) = 0. \]

Expanding,

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} K^{-\top}K^{-1} \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0. \]

Using

\[ \omega = K^{-\top}K^{-1}, \]

we obtain

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0. \]

This is exactly why we developed the absolute conic and its image: they turn the Euclidean structure hidden inside the camera into algebraic constraints on the unknown intrinsics.

This is the first constraint supplied by the homography.

Next, since \(\mathbf r_1\) and \(\mathbf r_2\) are both unit vectors,

\[ \|c\mathbf r_1\|_2^2 = \|c\mathbf r_2\|_2^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \left\|K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_1\right\|_2^2 = \left\|K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_2\right\|_2^2. \]

Expanding again,

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} K^{-\top}K^{-1} \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 = \widehat{\mathbf h}_2^{\top} K^{-\top}K^{-1} \widehat{\mathbf h}_2. \]

Hence,

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 - \widehat{\mathbf h}_2^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0. \]

This is the second constraint.

Thus one homography gives two equations involving \(\omega\):

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0, \]

and

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_1^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_1 - \widehat{\mathbf h}_2^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_2 = 0. \]

Now recall that \(\omega\) is symmetric:

\[ \omega = \begin{pmatrix} \omega_{11} & \omega_{12} & \omega_{13}\\ \omega_{12} & \omega_{22} & \omega_{23}\\ \omega_{13} & \omega_{23} & \omega_{33} \end{pmatrix}. \]

It has six entries. However, the equations used to estimate it are homogeneous, so \(\omega\) can initially be recovered only up to a common nonzero scale. It therefore has five independent degrees of freedom.

One homography provides only two constraints.

Therefore, a single planar image does not contain enough information to recover a completely general intrinsic matrix.

A second image of the same plane, viewed from a different pose, produces another homography and two more constraints. A third sufficiently different view produces two more.

The intrinsic matrix \(K\) remains the same across all views, while the rotation and translation change:

\[ H_i = K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_{1i} & \mathbf r_{2i} & \mathbf t_i \end{bmatrix}. \]

Each new homography contributes two equations involving the same matrix \(\omega\).

This is the central idea of planar camera calibration.

Combining several homographies

Suppose we have \(m\) different views of the same plane.

For view \(i\), we estimate a homography

\[ \widehat H_i = \begin{bmatrix} \widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i} & \widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i} & \widehat{\mathbf h}_{3i} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Each view gives the two constraints

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i}^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i} = 0, \]

and

\[ \widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i}^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i} - \widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i}^{\top} \omega \widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i} = 0. \]

The rotation and translation are different for every view, but the matrix \(\omega\) is the same because the intrinsic matrix \(K\) remains unchanged.

The next step is to write these constraints as a linear system in the unknown entries of \(\omega\).

Turning the constraints into linear equations

Write

\[ \omega = \begin{pmatrix} \omega_{11} & \omega_{12} & \omega_{13}\\ \omega_{12} & \omega_{22} & \omega_{23}\\ \omega_{13} & \omega_{23} & \omega_{33} \end{pmatrix}. \]

Collect its six independent entries into the vector

\[ \mathbf b = \begin{pmatrix} \omega_{11}\\ \omega_{12}\\ \omega_{22}\\ \omega_{13}\\ \omega_{23}\\ \omega_{33} \end{pmatrix}. \]

Now take two arbitrary vectors

\[ \mathbf a = \begin{pmatrix} a_1\\ a_2\\ a_3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf c = \begin{pmatrix} c_1\\ c_2\\ c_3 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf a^{\top}\omega\mathbf c \]

expands as

\[ \mathbf a^{\top}\omega\mathbf c = a_1c_1\omega_{11} + (a_1c_2+a_2c_1)\omega_{12} + a_2c_2\omega_{22} + (a_1c_3+a_3c_1)\omega_{13} + (a_2c_3+a_3c_2)\omega_{23} + a_3c_3\omega_{33}. \]

Although this expression is quadratic in the entries of \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf c\), it is linear in the unknown entries of \(\omega\).

Define

\[ \mathbf v(\mathbf a,\mathbf c) = \begin{pmatrix} a_1c_1\\ a_1c_2+a_2c_1\\ a_2c_2\\ a_1c_3+a_3c_1\\ a_2c_3+a_3c_2\\ a_3c_3 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf a^{\top}\omega\mathbf c = \mathbf v(\mathbf a,\mathbf c)^{\top}\mathbf b. \]

Applying this to the orthogonality constraint from view \(i\) gives

\[ \mathbf v \left( \widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i}, \widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i} \right)^{\top} \mathbf b = 0. \]

Applying it to the equal-length constraint gives

\[ \left[ \mathbf v \left( \widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i}, \widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i} \right) - \mathbf v \left( \widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i}, \widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i} \right) \right]^{\top} \mathbf b = 0. \]

Thus every view contributes two rows to a linear system.

For \(m\) views, we stack all the constraints:

\[ V\mathbf b = \mathbf 0, \]

where

\[ V \in \mathbb R^{2m\times 6}. \]

Explicitly,

\[ V = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf v(\widehat{\mathbf h}_{11},\widehat{\mathbf h}_{21})^{\top}\\ \left[ \mathbf v(\widehat{\mathbf h}_{11},\widehat{\mathbf h}_{11}) - \mathbf v(\widehat{\mathbf h}_{21},\widehat{\mathbf h}_{21}) \right]^{\top}\\ \vdots\\ \mathbf v(\widehat{\mathbf h}_{1m},\widehat{\mathbf h}_{2m})^{\top}\\ \left[ \mathbf v(\widehat{\mathbf h}_{1m},\widehat{\mathbf h}_{1m}) - \mathbf v(\widehat{\mathbf h}_{2m},\widehat{\mathbf h}_{2m}) \right]^{\top} \end{pmatrix}. \]

The nonlinear-looking geometric constraints have now become a homogeneous linear system.

Solving for the image of the absolute conic

In the ideal noiseless case, there exists a nonzero vector \(\mathbf b\) satisfying

\[ V\mathbf b = \mathbf 0. \]

Since \(\mathbf b\in\mathbb R^6\), a unique solution up to scale requires

\[ \dim\operatorname{Null}(V)=1. \]

By the rank-nullity theorem,

\[ \dim\operatorname{Null}(V) = 6-\operatorname{rank}(V). \]

Therefore, we want

\[ \operatorname{rank}(V)=5. \]

In that case,

\[ \operatorname{Null}(V) = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf b\}, \]

so every nonzero solution is a scalar multiple of the desired vector.

Each view contributes two rows. Therefore, at least three views are required in the general case:

\[ m\geq 3. \]

With three views,

\[ V\in\mathbb R^{6\times 6}, \]

but the matrix should have rank \(5\), not rank \(6\), because a nonzero null-space vector must exist.

Why use the singular value decomposition?

In exact arithmetic, we could compute the null space of \(V\) directly.

In real data, however, the estimated homographies contain noise. As a result, the constraints are not satisfied exactly, and \(V\) will usually have full numerical rank:

\[ \operatorname{rank}(V)=6. \]

Then the only exact solution to

\[ V\mathbf b=\mathbf 0 \]

is

\[ \mathbf b=\mathbf 0, \]

which is useless.

Instead, we search for the unit vector that violates the constraints as little as possible:

\[ \min_{\|\mathbf b\|_2=1} \|V\mathbf b\|_2^2. \]

Let

\[ V = U\Sigma W^{\top} \]

be the singular value decomposition of \(V\).

Write the right singular vectors as

\[ W = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf w_1 & \mathbf w_2 & \cdots & \mathbf w_6 \end{bmatrix}, \]

with singular values ordered as

\[ \sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_6 \geq 0. \]

The minimizer is the right singular vector corresponding to the smallest singular value:

\[ \widehat{\mathbf b} = \mathbf w_6. \]

To see why, write any unit vector as

\[ \mathbf b = \sum_{j=1}^{6} \alpha_j\mathbf w_j, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{6}\alpha_j^2=1. \]

Then

\[ \|V\mathbf b\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{6} \sigma_j^2\alpha_j^2. \]

This is minimized by placing all the weight on the smallest singular value:

\[ \alpha_6=1, \qquad \alpha_1=\cdots=\alpha_5=0. \]

Hence,

\[ \widehat{\mathbf b} = \mathbf w_6. \]

In the noiseless case,

\[ \sigma_6=0, \]

and \(\mathbf w_6\) spans the exact null space.

In the noisy case,

\[ \sigma_6>0, \]

but \(\mathbf w_6\) gives the best approximate null-space direction.

Recovering \(\omega\)

Once \(\widehat{\mathbf b}\) has been found, reshape it into the symmetric matrix

\[ \widehat\omega = \begin{pmatrix} \widehat b_1 & \widehat b_2 & \widehat b_4\\ \widehat b_2 & \widehat b_3 & \widehat b_5\\ \widehat b_4 & \widehat b_5 & \widehat b_6 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Because the system

\[ V\mathbf b=\mathbf 0 \]

is homogeneous, \(\widehat{\mathbf b}\) is recovered only up to a nonzero scale.

Therefore,

\[ \widehat\omega = \gamma\omega \]

for some nonzero scalar \(\gamma\).

This scale ambiguity will disappear when we normalize the recovered intrinsic matrix.

Recovering the intrinsic matrix

We know that

\[ \omega = K^{-\top}K^{-1}. \]

Taking inverses gives

\[ \omega^{-1} = KK^{\top}. \]

Because \(K\) is upper triangular, \(K^{\top}\) is lower triangular.

Suppose we compute the Cholesky factorization

\[ \omega^{-1} = LL^{\top}, \]

where \(L\) is lower triangular.

Comparing the two factorizations gives

\[ L = K^{\top}, \]

and therefore

\[ K = L^{\top}. \]

Because \(\widehat\omega\) is known only up to scale, the resulting matrix is also a scaled version of \(K\).

We remove that scale by enforcing the usual convention

\[ K_{33}=1. \]

Thus, if the factorization gives a matrix \(\widetilde K\), we normalize it as

\[ K = \frac{\widetilde K}{\widetilde K_{33}}. \]

We also choose the signs so that the focal lengths are positive:

\[ f_x>0, \qquad f_y>0. \]

At this point, the intrinsic matrix has been recovered. ## Recovering the pose of each view

Once \(K\) is known, we return to each homography separately.

For view \(i\),

\[ \widehat H_i = \begin{bmatrix} \widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i} & \widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i} & \widehat{\mathbf h}_{3i} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Recall that

\[ \widehat H_i = c_i K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_{1i} & \mathbf r_{2i} & \mathbf t_i \end{bmatrix} \]

for some nonzero multiplier \(c_i\). Therefore,

\[ K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i} = c_i\mathbf r_{1i}, \]

\[ K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i} = c_i\mathbf r_{2i}, \]

and

\[ K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_{3i} = c_i\mathbf t_i. \]

Since \(\mathbf r_{1i}\) and \(\mathbf r_{2i}\) are unit vectors, the scale can be estimated from their lengths.

With noisy data, the two lengths will generally not be exactly equal, so we use their average:

\[ \lambda_i = \frac{2} { \left\| K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i} \right\|_2 + \left\| K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i} \right\|_2 }. \]

We then obtain

\[ \mathbf r_{1i} = \lambda_i K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_{1i}, \]

\[ \mathbf r_{2i} = \lambda_i K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_{2i}, \]

and

\[ \mathbf t_i = \lambda_i K^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf h}_{3i}. \]

The third rotation column is obtained from

\[ \mathbf r_{3i} = \mathbf r_{1i}\times\mathbf r_{2i}. \]

This gives the approximate rotation matrix

\[ \widetilde R_i = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_{1i} & \mathbf r_{2i} & \mathbf r_{3i} \end{bmatrix}. \]

In exact arithmetic,

\[ \widetilde R_i^\top\widetilde R_i=I \]

and

\[ \det(\widetilde R_i)=1. \]

With noisy image measurements, however, \(\widetilde R_i\) will generally be only approximately orthogonal. We therefore replace it with the nearest valid rotation matrix.

Projecting onto the nearest rotation matrix

We seek a matrix \(Q\in SO(3)\) that minimizes

\[ \min_{Q\in SO(3)} \left\| \widetilde R_i-Q \right\|_F^2, \]

where

\[ SO(3) = \left\{ Q\in\mathbb R^{3\times3} : Q^\top Q=I, \quad \det(Q)=1 \right\}. \]

First consider the corresponding minimization over all orthogonal matrices:

\[ \min_{Q\in O(3)} \left\| \widetilde R_i-Q \right\|_F^2, \]

where

\[ O(3) = \left\{ Q\in\mathbb R^{3\times3} : Q^\top Q=I \right\}. \]

Here, \(O(3)\) denotes the orthogonal group in three dimensions: the set of all \(3\times 3\) orthogonal matrices. The \(3\) refers to the dimension of the underlying space, \(\mathbb R^3\).

Similarly, \(SO(3)\) denotes the special orthogonal group in three dimensions. The word special means that we restrict to orthogonal matrices with determinant \(+1\).

You do not need to understand the word group at this point. For now, think of simply as a technical term.

Expanding the Frobenius norm,

\[ \begin{aligned} \left\| \widetilde R_i-Q \right\|_F^2 &= \operatorname{tr} \left[ (\widetilde R_i-Q)^\top (\widetilde R_i-Q) \right]\\ &= \operatorname{tr} \left( \widetilde R_i^\top\widetilde R_i \right) + \operatorname{tr} \left( Q^\top Q \right) - 2\operatorname{tr} \left( Q^\top\widetilde R_i \right). \end{aligned} \]

Since \(Q^\top Q=I\),

\[ \operatorname{tr}(Q^\top Q)=3. \]

The first two terms do not depend on \(Q\). Therefore, minimizing the Frobenius distance is equivalent to maximizing

\[ \operatorname{tr} \left( Q^\top\widetilde R_i \right). \]

Let

\[ \widetilde R_i = U_i\Sigma_iV_i^\top \]

be the singular value decomposition of \(\widetilde R_i\), where

\[ \Sigma_i = \operatorname{diag} \left( \sigma_{1i}, \sigma_{2i}, \sigma_{3i} \right). \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr} \left( Q^\top\widetilde R_i \right) &= \operatorname{tr} \left( Q^\top U_i\Sigma_iV_i^\top \right)\\ &= \operatorname{tr} \left( V_i^\top Q^\top U_i\Sigma_i \right). \end{aligned} \]

Define

\[ M_i = U_i^\top QV_i. \]

Then

\[ M_i^\top = V_i^\top Q^\top U_i, \]

and hence

\[ \operatorname{tr} \left( Q^\top\widetilde R_i \right) = \operatorname{tr} \left( M_i^\top\Sigma_i \right). \]

Since \(M_i\) is orthogonal,

\[ -1\leq (M_i)_{jj}\leq 1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr} \left( M_i^\top\Sigma_i \right) &= \sigma_{1i}(M_i)_{11} + \sigma_{2i}(M_i)_{22} + \sigma_{3i}(M_i)_{33}\\ &\leq \sigma_{1i} + \sigma_{2i} + \sigma_{3i}. \end{aligned} \]

The upper bound is attained when

\[ M_i=I. \]

Thus,

\[ U_i^\top QV_i=I, \]

which gives

\[ Q=U_iV_i^\top. \]

If

\[ \det(U_iV_i^\top)=1, \]

then \(Q\) is already a valid rotation matrix, and we set

\[ R_i = U_iV_i^\top. \]

If instead

\[ \det(U_iV_i^\top)=-1, \]

then \(U_iV_i^\top\) is orthogonal but is not a rotation matrix.

In this case, define

\[ D_i = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&1&0\\ 0&0&-1 \end{pmatrix}, \]

and set

\[ R_i = U_iD_iV_i^\top. \]

Then

\[ R_i^\top R_i=I, \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \det(R_i) &= \det(U_iV_i^\top)\det(D_i)\\ &= (-1)(-1)\\ &= 1. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ R_i\in SO(3). \]

The final pose estimate for view \(i\) is

\[ R_i, \qquad \mathbf t_i. \]

Linear calibration is only an initialization

The procedure so far gives us estimates of

\[ K, \qquad R_i, \qquad \mathbf t_i. \]

These estimates were obtained through a sequence of linear steps:

\[ \text{point correspondences} \longrightarrow \widehat H_i \longrightarrow V \longrightarrow \widehat\omega \longrightarrow K. \]

But the quantity we ultimately care about is not the algebraic error

\[ \|V\mathbf b\|_2. \]

We care about how accurately the estimated camera predicts the observed image points.

For planar point \(j\) in view \(i\), let

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf X}_j = \begin{pmatrix} X_j\\ Y_j\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The predicted image point is

\[ \widehat{\mathbf x}_{ij} = \phi \left( K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_{1i} & \mathbf r_{2i} & \mathbf t_i \end{bmatrix} \widetilde{\mathbf X}_j \right). \]

If the observed image point is \(\mathbf x_{ij}\), the reprojection residual is

\[ \mathbf e_{ij} = \mathbf x_{ij} - \widehat{\mathbf x}_{ij}. \]

The final estimates are obtained by minimizing the total reprojection error:

\[ \min_{K,\{R_i,\mathbf t_i\}_{i=1}^{m}} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n_i} \left\| \mathbf x_{ij} - \phi \left( K \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r_{1i} & \mathbf r_{2i} & \mathbf t_i \end{bmatrix} \widetilde{\mathbf X}_j \right) \right\|_2^2. \]

This is a nonlinear least-squares problem.

The linear calibration method provides the starting point, and an iterative method such as Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt refines all the parameters jointly.

The important difference from refining a single camera matrix is that the intrinsic matrix is shared across every view:

\[ K_1=K_2=\cdots=K_m=K, \]

while every image has its own rotation and translation:

\[ R_i, \qquad \mathbf t_i. \]

Thus the optimization adjusts one common intrinsic matrix together with several image-specific poses.

NoteA note on rotations and Lie groups

A rotation matrix cannot be updated by changing its nine entries independently, because it must continue to satisfy

\[ R^{\top}R=I, \qquad \det(R)=1. \]

The set of all \(3\times3\) rotation matrices is called the special orthogonal group

\[ SO(3). \]

This is an example of a Lie group: a space that is simultaneously a smooth geometric object and a group.

This structure allows us to describe a small change in rotation using only three parameters and then convert that change back into a valid rotation matrix.

We will return to Lie groups later. For now, the important point is simply that rotations must be updated in a way that preserves their geometric structure.

The complete calibration pipeline is therefore

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{planar point correspondences} &\longrightarrow \widehat H_1,\ldots,\widehat H_m\\ &\longrightarrow V\mathbf b=\mathbf 0\\ &\longrightarrow \widehat\omega\\ &\longrightarrow K\\ &\longrightarrow R_i,\mathbf t_i\\ &\longrightarrow \text{nonlinear reprojection-error refinement}. \end{aligned} } \]

The remarkable part is that a flat plane, observed from several different poses, contains enough Euclidean information to reveal the internal geometry of the camera.

Lens distortion

The camera matrix

\[ P=K[R\mid\mathbf t] \]

describes an ideal pinhole camera.

For a world point \(\mathbf X_w\), the camera model predicts an ideal pixel location

\[ \mathbf x = \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix}. \]

A real lens may record the same point at a slightly different pixel location

\[ \mathbf x_d = \begin{pmatrix} x_d\\ y_d \end{pmatrix}. \]

Lens distortion models the difference between the ideal pinhole prediction \(\mathbf x\) and the observed distorted position \(\mathbf x_d\).

Radial distortion

The optical axis intersects the image at the principal point

\[ \mathbf p = \begin{pmatrix} p_x\\ p_y \end{pmatrix}. \]

The vector from the principal point to the ideal pixel is

\[ \mathbf x-\mathbf p = \begin{pmatrix} x-p_x\\ y-p_y \end{pmatrix}. \]

Its distance from the principal point is

\[ r = \|\mathbf x-\mathbf p\|_2 = \sqrt{(x-p_x)^2+(y-p_y)^2}. \]

Radial distortion assumes that the ideal point, the distorted point and the principal point remain on the same line. The lens changes only the distance of the point from the principal point.

We therefore write

\[ \mathbf x_d-\mathbf p = L(r)(\mathbf x-\mathbf p), \]

where \(L(r)\) is a radial scale factor.

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf x_d = \mathbf p+L(r)(\mathbf x-\mathbf p). } \]

A commonly used polynomial model is

\[ L(r) = 1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6. \]

Hence,

\[ x_d = p_x+ (x-p_x) \left( 1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6 \right), \]

and

\[ y_d = p_y+ (y-p_y) \left( 1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6 \right). \]

The distorted radial distance is

\[ r_d=L(r)r. \]

Therefore:

  • if \(L(r)=1\), the point does not move;
  • if \(L(r)<1\), the point moves towards the principal point;
  • if \(L(r)>1\), the point moves away from the principal point.

Since \(r\) is small near the principal point,

\[ L(r)\approx 1 \]

there. The distortion generally becomes more visible towards the image boundaries.

Why are the odd powers missing from \(L(r)\)?

Consider any line passing through the principal point. Let \(t\) be a signed coordinate along this line, with \(t=0\) at the principal point.

Let \(g(t)\) be the distorted signed coordinate. Radial symmetry requires opposite points to remain opposite:

\[ g(-t)=-g(t). \]

Thus \(g\) is an odd function. Assuming it is smooth, its Taylor expansion contains only odd powers:

\[ g(t) = a_1t+a_3t^3+a_5t^5+a_7t^7+\cdots. \]

Taking the first-order scale to be one,

\[ g(t) = t+k_1t^3+k_2t^5+k_3t^7+\cdots. \]

Factoring out \(t\) gives

\[ g(t) = t \left( 1+k_1t^2+k_2t^4+k_3t^6+\cdots \right). \]

The expression inside the parentheses is the radial scale factor. Replacing the signed coordinate by the radial distance gives

\[ L(r) = 1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6+\cdots. \]

Thus, the full radial mapping contains odd powers,

\[ r_d = r+k_1r^3+k_2r^5+k_3r^7+\cdots, \]

while the multiplicative scale factor contains even powers because one factor of \(r\) has been factored out:

\[ L(r) = \frac{r_d}{r} = 1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6+\cdots. \]

Barrel and pincushion distortion

Under the forward distortion model

\[ \mathbf x_d-\mathbf p = L(r)(\mathbf x-\mathbf p), \]

if points away from the principal point are moved inward, straight lines near the image boundary bow outward. This is called barrel distortion.

For the simple model

\[ L(r)=1+k_1r^2, \]

this usually corresponds to

\[ k_1<0. \]

If points are moved outward from the principal point, straight lines near the image boundary bend inward. This is called pincushion distortion.

Under the same convention, this usually corresponds to

\[ k_1>0. \]

Different points on an ideal straight line generally lie at different radial distances from the principal point. They therefore receive different scale factors:

\[ L(r_1),L(r_2),\ldots. \]

Because the points are displaced by different amounts, they need not remain collinear. This is why straight lines can appear curved in a distorted image.

Lens distortion is nonlinear and therefore cannot, in general, be absorbed into the camera matrix \(P\).

Back to camera calibration

The homography-based method gives initial estimates of

\[ K, \qquad R_i, \qquad \mathbf t_i \]

for every view \(i\).

We may initialize the distortion coefficients as

\[ k_1=k_2=k_3=0. \]

These parameters are then refined jointly by minimizing the reprojection error over all observed corners and all images:

\[ \min_{K,\{R_i,\mathbf t_i\}_{i=1}^{m},k_1,k_2,k_3} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n_i} \left\| \mathbf x_{ij} - \widehat{\mathbf x}_{ij} \right\|_2^2, \]

where \(\mathbf x_{ij}\) is the observed pixel location and \(\widehat{\mathbf x}_{ij}\) is the location predicted by the current camera and distortion parameters.

\[ \widehat{\mathbf x}_{ij} = D \left( \phi \left( K \begin{bmatrix} R_i & \mathbf t_i \end{bmatrix} \widetilde{\mathbf X}_{w,j} \right) \right), \]

where

\[ D(\mathbf x) = \mathbf p + L(r)(\mathbf x-\mathbf p), \]

with

\[ \mathbf p = \begin{pmatrix} p_x\\ p_y \end{pmatrix}, \qquad r = \|\mathbf x-\mathbf p\|_2, \]

and

\[ L(r) = 1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6. \]

Equivalently, define the ideal pinhole projection

\[ \pi_i(\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{w,j}) = \phi \left( K \begin{bmatrix} R_i & \mathbf t_i \end{bmatrix} \widetilde{\mathbf X}_{w,j} \right). \]

Then the predicted distorted pixel is simply

\[ \boxed{ \widehat{\mathbf x}_{ij} = D \left( \pi_i(\widetilde{\mathbf X}_{w,j}) \right). } \]

As discussed in Part 3, this nonlinear least-squares problem can be solved using Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt.

The complete calibration pipeline is therefore

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{planar correspondences} &\longrightarrow \widehat H_1,\ldots,\widehat H_m\\ &\longrightarrow \widehat\omega\\ &\longrightarrow K\\ &\longrightarrow R_i,\mathbf t_i\\ &\longrightarrow \text{joint reprojection-error refinement}. \end{aligned} } \]

The linear stage provides a geometrically meaningful initialization. The nonlinear stage then refines the intrinsic parameters, the pose of every view and the lens-distortion coefficients together.

Conclusion

We began with the image of the absolute conic,

\[ \omega = K^{-\top}K^{-1}, \]

and asked how it could be estimated from actual images.

A planar calibration target gave us the missing link. Each image of the plane produced a homography, and the first two columns of that homography inherited the orthogonality and equal-length constraints of the first two rotation columns.

Expressed through \(\omega\), these became linear equations in the unknown entries of the intrinsic matrix.

By combining constraints from several views, we formed the homogeneous system

\[ V\mathbf b = \mathbf 0, \]

solved it using the SVD, reconstructed \(\omega\), and recovered \(K\) through a Cholesky factorization.

Once \(K\) was known, each homography could be decomposed to estimate the pose of the calibration plane relative to the camera.

Because image measurements are noisy, the recovered rotation matrices were only approximately valid rotations. We therefore replaced each one with the nearest matrix in \(SO(3)\).

Finally, we introduced radial lens distortion and used the linear estimates as the initialization for a nonlinear reprojection-error minimization that jointly refines the camera intrinsics, the pose of every view, and the distortion coefficients.

The complete calibration pipeline is therefore

\[ \text{checkerboard images} \longrightarrow \text{corner correspondences} \longrightarrow \text{homographies} \longrightarrow \omega \longrightarrow K \longrightarrow \{R_i,\mathbf t_i\} \longrightarrow \text{nonlinear refinement}. \]

What began as the abstract matrix

\[ \omega = K^{-\top}K^{-1} \]

has now become something measurable.

A collection of photographs of a flat checkerboard is enough to recover the internal geometry of the camera.

What comes next?

Calibration tells us how a single camera maps points in the three-dimensional world to points in an image.

The next question is what happens when the same scene is observed by two cameras, or by the same camera from two different positions.

A point in the first image cannot correspond to an arbitrary point in the second image. The camera geometry restricts its possible match to a line.

This is the beginning of epipolar geometry.

In the next part, we will derive the epipolar constraint from the two camera matrices and arrive at the fundamental relation

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top F \widetilde{\mathbf x}_1 = 0. \]

We will then ask what the matrix \(F\) contains, how it is related to the calibrated-camera matrix \(E\), and how two images begin to reveal the three-dimensional structure of a scene.
